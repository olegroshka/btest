% ── Abstract ──────────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{abstract}
\noindent
We develop a spectral state-space framework for forecasting cross-sectional investment intensity. The framework extracts a low-dimensional ($K = 2$) spectral basis via Dynamic Mode Decomposition (DMD) and filters latent modal states through a Kalman filter with dual regularisation: spherical observation covariance ($R = cI$) and near-identity transition matrix ($F = 0.99I$). On a 146-firm US CapEx/Revenue panel under nested cross-validation (frozen holdout 2023--2024), the rolling-basis variant achieves predictive $R^2 = 0.711$ (8 CV windows; $\Delta R^2 = +0.042$ vs per-actor AR(1); bootstrap CI $[+0.031, +0.055]$; exact permutation $p = 0.004$; 8/8 windows) and $R^2 = 0.845$ on 2 holdout windows. Replacing DMD with PCA (same pipeline otherwise) yields weaker gains ($\Delta R^2 = +0.001$ under nested CV; $+0.021$ at fixed $T = 2$yr), suggesting the advantage is consistent with DMD's temporal snapshot formulation rather than cross-sectional pooling alone; a truncated SVD PCA baseline rules out implicit SVD regularisation as the source (Section~\ref{sec:baselines}). The advantage increases monotonically as the training window shortens---$\Delta R^2 = +0.057$ at $T = 2$~years (fixed $K\!=\!2$, $\tau\!=\!12$Q; CI $[+0.042, +0.074]$; 10/10 windows)---consistent with cross-sectional pooling. On a 93-actor structural panel ($K = 8$), the spectral basis rotates at $25.8^\circ$/quarter ($\sigma = 6.5^\circ$; null simulation: $75.3^\circ$); ablation confirms that dual regularisation and rolling basis update are essential for spectral reconstruction quality, with rolling update providing the largest modal $R^2$ gain ($+14.7$pp, CI $[+11.1, +17.2]$pp). On the predictive panel, exponentially-weighted mean (EWM) demeaning provides the largest single forecast gain ($+3.5$pp), with rolling basis update adding $+3.4$pp. Predictive model-implied investment gaps are associated with subsequent cross-sectional CapEx-rank revision ($t \approx -6.4$ to $-7.1$ with clustered SEs; cross-sectional effect that reverses under actor fixed effects).
\end{abstract}

\vspace{0.3cm}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

Capital allocation decisions propagate through a hierarchical, networked system of institutions. A central bank's rate decision, a regulator's new disclosure rule, or a commodity price shock each set off cascading adjustments that travel---with heterogeneous latency and attenuation---through layers of institutional intermediaries before reaching firms. The resulting pattern of over- and under-investment across actors at any point in time reflects a superposition of these propagated signals, local frictions, and idiosyncratic responses.

Despite rich literatures on factor models \citep{fama1993common}, network effects in economics \citep{acemoglu2012network}, and spectral graph theory \citep{ortega2018graph}, to our knowledge no established framework jointly: (i)~decomposes how investment-relevant signals propagate through a heterogeneous actor hierarchy; (ii)~estimates actor-specific investment gaps as deviations from a structurally-informed benchmark; and (iii)~validates that the resulting gap estimates carry out-of-sample predictive content.

This paper addresses all three objectives. We develop \textbf{SMIM} (\textbf{S}pectral \textbf{M}odal \textbf{I}nvestment \textbf{M}odel), a state-space framework that represents the cross-sectional dynamics of investment intensity through a low-dimensional modal basis extracted via Dynamic Mode Decomposition (DMD), filtered through a Kalman model with dual regularisation at both the observation and transition levels. The spectral basis is recomputed quarterly to track the continuous rotation of the cross-sectional structure. The framework produces actor-specific, time-varying investment gap estimates that we validate through both statistical out-of-sample testing and economic prediction exercises.

\subsection{Preview of Results}

Throughout, we measure forecast performance by the out-of-sample coefficient of determination,
\begin{equation}
R^2 = 1 - \frac{\sum_{i,t}(y_{i,t} - \hat{y}_{i,t})^2}{\sum_{i,t}(y_{i,t} - \bar{y})^2},
\label{eq:r2}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{y}_{i,t}$ is the model's prediction and $\bar{y}$ is the test-period grand mean. $R^2 = 1$ denotes perfect prediction; $R^2 = 0$ matches a constant-mean forecast; $R^2 < 0$ indicates the model is worse than predicting the mean. The summation runs over all actor--quarter pairs in each test window.

Our main findings are:

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Forecast performance.} On a 146-firm US CapEx/Revenue panel with $K = 2$ spectral modes, the rolling-basis variant achieves predictive $R^2 = 0.711$ under nested CV (8 windows; $\Delta R^2 = +0.042$ vs AR(1); bootstrap CI $[+0.031, +0.055]$; exact permutation $p = 0.004$) and $R^2 = 0.845$ on 2 frozen holdout windows. A PCA baseline using the identical pipeline but replacing DMD with PCA eigendecomposition achieves $R^2 = 0.702$---statistically indistinguishable from its matched AR(1) ($\Delta R^2 = +0.001$, $p > 0.4$)---suggesting the advantage is consistent with DMD's temporal snapshot formulation rather than cross-sectional pooling alone; a truncated SVD PCA baseline rules out implicit SVD regularisation as the source (Section~\ref{sec:baselines}). The framework's advantage over AR(1) increases monotonically as the training window shortens (fixed $K\!=\!2$, $\tau\!=\!12$Q), reaching $\Delta R^2 = +0.057$ at $T = 2$~years (10/10 windows; CI $[+0.042, +0.074]$; Figure~\ref{fig:tsweep}). Inference in Table~\ref{tab:inference}.

\item \textbf{Dual regularisation.} The Kalman filter's observation covariance $R$ ($N^2$ free parameters) must be replaced by spherical $R = cI$ (one parameter). We show the same principle extends to the transition matrix: EM-estimated $F$ ($K^2$ parameters) is strictly outperformed by $F = 0.99I$ (one parameter) in all 10 windows. Online state-noise adaptation ($Q$) handles all temporal dynamics. Together, this \emph{dual regularisation} reduces the state-space model from $N^2 + K^2$ free parameters to 2 scalars.

\item \textbf{Basis rotation.} On the 93-actor structural panel ($K = 8$), the estimated spectral basis rotates at $25.8^\circ$ per quarter ($\sigma = 6.5^\circ$) while maintaining stable dimensionality across 52 quarterly transitions. A null simulation shows that estimation noise alone would produce $75.3^\circ$ of apparent rotation, suggesting the observed pattern reflects recoverable structure. Rolling basis update captures this rotation, yielding $+14.7$pp of modal $R^2$ (CI $[+11.1, +17.2]$pp, 100\% of windows).

\item \textbf{DMD over static operators.} Dynamic Mode Decomposition outperforms all static symmetric-operator methods (which produce identical results due to operator symmetry) by $+2.0$ percentage points on average (Section~\ref{sec:spectral}).

\item \textbf{Economic content.} Predictive model-implied investment gaps (using $\balpha_{t|t-1}$, which does not incorporate the current observation) are associated with subsequent cross-sectional CapEx-rank revision ($\hat{\beta} = -0.28$, $t = -6.4$ with actor-clustered SEs after controlling for intensity level). The result is cross-sectional: it flips sign under actor fixed effects (Table~\ref{tab:capex_reg}). Additional exploratory results on gap dispersion and market volatility are reported in Section~\ref{sec:economic} but are not part of the paper's main claims.

\item \textbf{Dynamics stability.} The transition parameters $(F, Q)$ need not be estimated per window: fixed $F = 0.99I$, $Q = 0.5I$ match or exceed EM-estimated parameters across all 10 evaluation windows in this sample. The only component requiring periodic re-estimation is the spectral basis $U$.

\item \textbf{Multi-signal generality.} The framework is not specific to CapEx/Revenue: Revenue/Assets (259 firms, $\Delta R^2 = +0.010$, 8/10 windows) and a multi-ratio panel stacking both constructions (270 firms, $\Delta R^2 = +0.028$, 9/10 windows) also beat AR(1) at $K = 2$ (Table~\ref{tab:multi_signal}). The framework requires sufficient cross-sectional dynamics (low persistence) and works on two additional low-persistence ratio constructions in this sample.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent Equally important is what \emph{does not} work: emergence diagnostics, directed spectral operators, event-level alignment, and return-based intensity all fail to improve or actively degrade performance. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} documents these negative results in detail.

\subsection{Scope and Evaluation Caveats}

Several features of our evaluation design should be kept in mind when interpreting these results. First, the out-of-sample evaluation uses 10 non-overlapping annual test windows, each containing only 4 quarterly observations; per-window $R^2$ estimates are therefore noisy (cross-window standard deviation $\approx 0.05$--$0.09$). Second, all hyperparameters ($K$, EWM halflife, $T$) are selected via nested rolling cross-validation: inner folds within each outer-train window select these values, with the final 2 windows (2023--2024) held out entirely from all tuning (Section~\ref{sec:data}). Third, the headline predictive result is on a 146-firm US panel using CapEx/Revenue intensity---return-based intensity produces negative $R^2$ on the same actors (Section~\ref{sec:transfer}). Fourth, the investment gap is a \emph{model-implied} deviation from a benchmark prediction, not a welfare-theoretic or structural misallocation measure in the \citet{hsieh2009misallocation} sense.

\subsection{Contribution}

Our contributions are methodological, empirical, and practical:

\textbf{Methodological.} We apply \emph{dual regularisation} to state-space models in high-dimensional panels: spherical observation covariance ($R = cI$, reducing $N^2$ parameters to 1) combined with near-identity transition regularisation ($F = 0.99I$, reducing $K^2$ parameters to 1). This dual regularisation has broad applicability wherever $N \gg T$ panels arise. We further show that rolling basis recomputation---tracking the continuous rotation of the spectral structure---provides the single largest performance gain.

\textbf{Empirical.} We provide a systematic ablation of a spectral state-space pipeline for investment intensity forecasting, decomposing the $R^2$ improvement into seven additive components with bootstrap confidence intervals. We observe that the cross-sectional spectral basis rotates at $25.8^\circ$ per quarter ($\sigma = 6.5^\circ$) while maintaining fixed dimensionality; a null simulation ($75.3^\circ$) confirms this is below finite-sample noise levels, suggesting recoverable low-dimensional structure.

\textbf{Practical.} Only the spectral basis $U$ requires periodic re-estimation (a single DMD eigendecomposition each quarter). The transition dynamics use stable default scalars ($F = 0.99I$, $Q = 0.5I$) that match or exceed EM-estimated parameters across all 10 evaluation windows in our sample, eliminating EM estimation of the transition dynamics and reducing operational complexity to one matrix factorisation plus a single scalar-$R$ EM pass per quarter.

\subsection{Related Literature}

Our work connects to several strands. The investment gap concept relates to the capital allocation literature, including the structural misallocation framework of \citet{hsieh2009misallocation} and the firm-level analysis of \citet{restuccia2017causes}; we note that our model-implied gap is a forecasting-based deviation, not a structural measure in their sense. Our spectral approach builds on signal processing on graphs \citep{ortega2018graph,sandryhaila2013discrete} and Dynamic Mode Decomposition \citep{schmid2010dynamic,kutz2016dynamic}, adapted here for economic panel data rather than fluid dynamics or power systems. The state-space filtering component extends the dynamic factor model tradition \citep{stock2002forecasting,doz2012quasi} with the regularisation insights of \citet{ledoit2004well} applied to the observation rather than factor covariance. The economic validation relates to the capital allocation efficiency literature \citep{wurgler2000financial} and the predictability of corporate investment \citep{abel1983optimal}.

\section{Framework}
\label{sec:framework}
% ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

\subsection{Setup}

Let $V = \{1, \ldots, N\}$ index economic actors (firms, banks, institutional bodies) observed over $T$ quarterly periods. Each actor $i$ has an observed \emph{investment intensity} $y_{i,t} \in [0,1]$, constructed as a cross-sectional percentile rank of a type-specific investment measure (Section~\ref{sec:data}). The $N \times T$ panel $Y = [y_{i,t}]$ is the primary input.

\begin{definition}[Investment Gap]
\label{def:gap}
The investment gap for actor $i$ at time $t$ relative to a benchmark functional $\mathcal{B}$ is
\begin{equation}
\Delta_{i,t} = y_{i,t} - y^*_{i,t}, \qquad y^*_{i,t} = \mathcal{B}(Y_{1:t}, \theta),
\label{eq:gap}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta_{i,t} > 0$ indicates investment above the model-implied benchmark and $\Delta_{i,t} < 0$ indicates investment below the benchmark, and $Y_{1:t}$ denotes the information set available at time $t$.
\end{definition}

The framework constructs $y^*_{i,t}$ through five sequential stages plus a rolling basis update, each justified by ablation (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}). Figure~\ref{fig:conceptual} provides an overview: panel~(a) shows the multilayer actor hierarchy with empirically-identified transmission channels; panel~(b) shows the static processing pipeline. The rolling basis extension is described in Section~\ref{sec:rolling}.

\subsection{Stage 1: Exponentially-Weighted Demeaning}
\label{sec:demean}

Investment intensity ranks exhibit strong cross-sectional persistence: an actor's mean rank is the single largest source of variance (51\% of total $R^2$ in the static-basis configuration; Section~\ref{sec:variance_decomp}). The state-space model $\tilde{y}_t = U\balpha_t + \varepsilon_t$ assumes zero-mean observations. Without demeaning, the modal states $\balpha_t$ must absorb the $O(0.5)$ mean through a basis $U$ with entries $O(N^{-1/2})$---an impossible task at $K \ll N$.

We subtract an exponentially-weighted per-actor mean:
\begin{equation}
\hat{\mu}_i = \frac{\sum_{s=1}^{T} w_s \, y_{i,s}}{\sum_{s=1}^{T} w_s}, \qquad w_s = \exp\!\Bigl(-\frac{(T - s) \ln 2}{\tau}\Bigr),
\label{eq:ewm}
\end{equation}
with half-life $\tau$ quarters ($\tau = 8$ for the structural analysis panel; $\tau \in \{8, 12\}$ selected by inner CV on the CapEx/Revenue panel). The demeaned observation is $\tilde{y}_{i,t} = y_{i,t} - \hat{\mu}_i$. Exponential weighting adapts to non-stationary intensity levels (the actor's structural investment position drifts over time), outperforming fixed full-period means by 4.2 percentage points in modal $R^2$ (Section~\ref{sec:results}). A fine grid over $\tau \in \{6, 7, \ldots, 14\}$ at fixed $K = 2$, $T = 3$yr shows a broad optimum near $\tau = 7$--$8$Q ($\Delta R^2 = +0.039$), with performance declining smoothly to $+0.030$ at $\tau = 6$ and $+0.034$ at $\tau = 12$--$14$.

At prediction time, the one-step-ahead forecast is $\hat{y}_{i,t} = \hat{\mu}_i + U \balpha_{t|t-1}$ (predictive). The investment gap (Eq.~\ref{eq:gap_compute}) also uses the predictive state $\balpha_{t|t-1}$. The modal reconstruction $\hat{\mu}_i + U \balpha_{t|t}$ incorporates the current observation and is used for spectral reconstruction quality (modal $R^2$) on the structural panel.

\subsection{Stage 2: Dynamic Mode Decomposition}
\label{sec:dmd}

Given the demeaned training panel $\tilde{Y} = [\tilde{y}_1, \ldots, \tilde{y}_T] \in \R^{N \times T}$, we extract a spectral basis that captures temporal cross-sectional dynamics.

\textbf{Why DMD over static operators.} Classical spectral approaches decompose a static operator (e.g., the cross-correlation matrix $C = \tilde{Y}\tilde{Y}^\top / T$). This captures contemporaneous co-movement but not lagged cross-sectional dynamics. Dynamic Mode Decomposition \citep{schmid2010dynamic} bypasses the static operator entirely by working with temporal snapshot pairs, capturing lagged cross-sectional dynamics that are invisible to purely contemporaneous decompositions.

\textbf{Connection to econometric reduced-rank models.} DMD can be viewed as learning a reduced-rank linear operator from temporal snapshot pairs: given $X' \approx AX$, the rank-$K$ DMD approximation is the best rank-$K$ approximation to $A$ in the Frobenius norm. This is closely related to reduced-rank regression \citep{reinsel1998multivariate} and the principal-component forecasting framework of \citet{stock2002forecasting} used in macroeconometrics, where a high-dimensional VAR(1) $y_{t+1} = Ay_t + \varepsilon_t$ is approximated by a low-rank factorisation $A \approx U\Lambda V^\top$. The key difference is that DMD uses the SVD of $X$ to project $A$ into a low-dimensional subspace before eigendecomposition, whereas reduced-rank VAR typically uses maximum likelihood or Stein-type shrinkage. In favorable low-rank settings, the leading subspaces recovered by the two approaches may be similar.

\textbf{DMD algorithm.} Form snapshot matrices $X = [\tilde{y}_1, \ldots, \tilde{y}_{T-1}]$ and $X' = [\tilde{y}_2, \ldots, \tilde{y}_T]$. DMD seeks the best-fit linear operator $A$ such that $X' \approx AX$. Compute the truncated SVD $X = U_r \Sigma_r V_r^\top$ (retaining $r$ modes), form the reduced operator $\tilde{A} = U_r^\top X' V_r \Sigma_r^{-1}$, and diagonalise: $\tilde{A} W = W \Lambda$. The DMD modes are $\Phi = X' V_r \Sigma_r^{-1} W \in \R^{N \times K}$.

We retain $K$ modes, where $K$ is selected via nested cross-validation: within each outer-train fold, the last 2 years serve as inner-validation to choose $K \in \{2, 3\}$ (Section~\ref{sec:data}). On the CapEx/Revenue panel, inner CV selects $K = 2$ in 7/8 folds---the cross-sectional investment structure is genuinely 2-dimensional. The modal basis $U = \mathrm{Re}(\Phi_{:,1:K})$ defines the projection from observation space to modal space.

\textbf{Note on orthonormality.} The raw DMD modes $\Phi$ are not in general orthonormal ($U^\top U \neq I$). In the implementation, the Kalman filter uses $U$ as-is; the projection $U\balpha$ is a linear combination of (possibly non-orthogonal) modes, not a strict orthogonal projection. The projection-only PCA baseline (Section~\ref{sec:baselines}) orthonormalises via QR before projection to ensure $U U^\top$ is a true projector.

\subsection{Stage 3: Kalman Filter with Spherical Observation Covariance}
\label{sec:kalman}

Given the modal basis $U \in \R^{N \times K}$, we model the latent modal dynamics as a linear Gaussian state-space system:
\begin{align}
\balpha_{t+1} &= F \balpha_t + \eta_t, \qquad \eta_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, Q), \label{eq:state}\\
\tilde{y}_t &= U \balpha_t + \varepsilon_t, \qquad \varepsilon_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, R). \label{eq:obs}
\end{align}

The observation noise $R \in \R^{N \times N}$ is estimated via a single EM pass to obtain its scale. The transition matrix $F$ and state noise $Q$ are \emph{not} estimated by EM---they are set to regularised defaults (see below).

\textbf{The overparameterisation problem.} The observation covariance $R$ has $N(N+1)/2$ free parameters---$4{,}278$ for the 93-actor structural panel, $10{,}731$ for the 146-firm CapEx/Revenue panel---estimated from $T = 8$--$20$ quarterly observations. This extreme $N \gg T$ setting causes the EM to converge to an ill-conditioned $R$ that overfits training noise (modal $R^2 = -7.3$ without regularisation on the structural panel; Figure~\ref{fig:reg}).

\textbf{Spherical observation regularisation.} We replace the full $R$ with
\begin{equation}
R_{\mathrm{sph}} = \frac{\tr(\hat{R})}{N} \, I_N,
\label{eq:spherical}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{R}$ is the EM-estimated sample covariance. This reduces the parameter count from $O(N^2)$ to $O(1)$.

\textbf{Transition regularisation.} The same overparameterisation principle applies to the transition matrix $F \in \R^{K \times K}$. Even at low $K$ ($K = 2$--$3$ for the headline panel), EM estimation of $F$ overfits to the training alpha trajectory. On the structural panel ($K = 8$), this costs $-1.9$~percentage points of modal $R^2$ relative to a near-identity prior. We therefore set
\begin{equation}
F = 0.99 \, I_K, \qquad Q_0 = 0.5 \, I_K,
\label{eq:f_reg}
\end{equation}
and rely on the online $Q$ adaptation (Stage~4) to capture all temporal dynamics. This \emph{dual regularisation}---spherical $R$ (1 parameter from $N^2$) and near-identity $F$ (1 parameter from $K^2$)---reduces the state-space model to two scalars plus the spectral basis $U$, which carries all the structural information.

The impact on the structural panel is dramatic: modal $R^2$ swings from $-7.3$ (no regularisation) through $+0.43$ (spherical $R$ only) to $+0.549$ (dual regularisation), establishing that covariance regularisation at \emph{both} the observation and transition levels is essential in high-dimensional panels.

\subsection{Stage 4: Online State-Noise Adaptation}
\label{sec:online}

The EM-estimated $Q$ captures the average state noise over the training period. When the test period exhibits different volatility (e.g., a regime shift), the fixed $Q$ misspecifies the state dynamics. We augment the standard Kalman recursion with a recursive $Q$ update:
\begin{equation}
Q_{t+1} = (1 - \lambda)\, Q_t + \lambda\, (\balpha_{t|t} - F\balpha_{t-1|t-1})(\balpha_{t|t} - F\balpha_{t-1|t-1})^\top, \qquad \lambda = 0.3.
\label{eq:online_q}
\end{equation}

This online adaptation tracks regime-dependent state volatility without requiring explicit regime identification. Combined with the spherical $R$ (which remains fixed), the joint step of adding online $Q$ and increasing $K$ from 5 to 8 adds $+5.8$ percentage points to modal $R^2$ in the ablation ladder (Table~\ref{tab:ladder}); the gain cannot be attributed solely to online $Q$ because $K$ changes simultaneously.

\subsection{Stage 5: Gap Computation}

The investment gap for actor $i$ at test time $t$ is:
\begin{equation}
\Delta_{i,t} = y_{i,t} - \underbrace{(\hat{\mu}_i + U\balpha_{t|t-1})}_{\displaystyle y^*_{i,t}},
\label{eq:gap_compute}
\end{equation}
where $\balpha_{t|t-1} = F\balpha_{t-1|t-1}$ is the Kalman-predicted modal state (using only information up to $t-1$, before observing $\tilde{y}_t$). This is the same predictive state used for forecast evaluation, ensuring the gap is a genuine ex-ante deviation.

\subsection{Summary: The Recommended Pipeline}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:pipeline} summarises the complete procedure.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{SMIM: Spectral Modal Investment Gap Estimation (Rolling Variant)}
\label{alg:pipeline}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Intensity panel $Y \in [0,1]^{N \times T}$, initial training window, $K$ (inner CV; typically 2), $\tau$ (inner CV; typically 8--12Q), $\lambda = 0.3$
\Ensure Investment gaps $\Delta_{i,t}$ for test period
\State Compute EWM mean $\hat{\mu}_i$ from training period \Comment{Eq.~\eqref{eq:ewm}}
\State Demean: $\tilde{Y} = Y - \hat{\mu}$
\State Extract DMD modes $U \in \R^{N \times K}$ from training $\tilde{Y}$ \Comment{Section~\ref{sec:dmd}}
\State Set $F = 0.99\, I_K$, $\;Q = 0.5\, I_K$ \Comment{Regularised, no EM; Eq.~\eqref{eq:f_reg}}
\State Estimate $\hat{R}$ via single Kalman EM pass; set $R = \frac{\tr(\hat{R})}{N} I_N$ \Comment{Eq.~\eqref{eq:spherical}}
\For{each test quarter $t$}
  \State Kalman predict: $\balpha_{t|t-1} = F\balpha_{t-1|t-1}$
  \State \textbf{Forecast}: $\hat{y}_{i,t} = \hat{\mu}_i + U\balpha_{t|t-1}$ \Comment{Predictive; for evaluation}
  \State \textbf{Predictive gap}: $\Delta_{i,t} = y_{i,t} - (\hat{\mu}_i + U\balpha_{t|t-1})$ \Comment{Ex-ante; Eq.~\eqref{eq:gap_compute}}
  \State Kalman update: $\balpha_{t|t}$ incorporating $\tilde{y}_t$, using $R$ \Comment{Modal state for structural analysis}
  \State Innovation: $\mathrm{innov}_t = \balpha_{t|t} - F\balpha_{t-1|t-1}$
  \State Adapt: $Q \leftarrow (1-\lambda)Q + \lambda\,\mathrm{innov}_t\,\mathrm{innov}_t^\top$ \Comment{Eq.~\eqref{eq:online_q}}
  \State \textbf{Rolling update:} expand training to include $y_t$; recompute $U$, $\hat{\mu}$, $R$; set $\balpha \leftarrow (U_{\mathrm{new}}^\top U_{\mathrm{new}})^{-1} U_{\mathrm{new}}^\top \tilde{y}_t$; reset $P$, $Q$, $F$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Investment Intensity}

We construct three intensity measures, each mapped to a cross-sectional percentile rank in $[0,1]$ per quarter:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{CapEx/Assets intensity} (structural analysis): the ratio of capital expenditures to total assets, sourced from SEC EDGAR quarterly filings (eXtensible Business Reporting Language (XBRL) tags \texttt{Payments\-To\-Acquire\-Property\-Plant\-And\-Equipment} / \texttt{Assets}), ranked cross-sectionally within each quarter. Used on the 93-actor panel for ablation and structural analysis.

\item \textbf{CapEx/Revenue intensity} (headline forecast): the ratio of capital expenditures to revenues, sourced from the same EDGAR tags (\texttt{Payments\-To\-Acquire\-Property\-Plant\-And\-Equipment} / \texttt{Revenues}), ranked cross-sectionally. This 146-firm US panel has lower persistence (median $\rho = 0.28$ vs $0.47$ for CapEx/Assets), leaving more room for cross-sectional spectral dynamics. All headline predictive results use this construction.

\item \textbf{Return intensity} (alternative): trailing 12-month equity total return, ranked cross-sectionally. Used for UK equities, which lack EDGAR coverage.

\item \textbf{Institutional intensity}: FRED-based macro indices (e.g., policy rates, commodity prices), min-max normalised within actor type.
\end{itemize}

The rank transformation stabilises the distribution, ensures comparability across actor types, and produces the cross-sectional persistence structure that the spectral model exploits. The intensity construction choice is consequential: on the 93-actor structural panel, CapEx/Assets intensity produces modal $R^2 = 0.54$ while return intensity produces modal $R^2 = -0.15$ on the same US actors (Section~\ref{sec:transfer}), establishing the measurement input as the primary driver of model performance.

Table~\ref{tab:sources} summarises the data sources. Notably, the pipeline uses \emph{only the intensity panel} for operator construction and prediction. External signal sources (OHLCV, FRED, EDGAR balance sheet) were tested as Granger-edge inputs but found dispensable (Section~\ref{sec:robustness}): the intensity cross-correlation captures all exploitable structure.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}

We employ a \textbf{nested rolling-validation} protocol with 10 non-overlapping annual test windows (2015--2024):

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Outer folds} (10 windows): final reported performance, one per test year.
\item \textbf{Inner folds}: within each outer-train, the last 2 years serve as inner validation to select $K \in \{2, 3\}$, EWM halflife $\tau \in \{8, 12\}$~quarters, and training length $T \in \{3, 5\}$ years (8 candidate configurations). The fixed defaults ($F = 0.99I$, $Q_0 = 0.5I$, $\lambda = 0.3$) are not tuned.
\item \textbf{Frozen holdout}: the final 2 windows (2023--2024) receive no inner tuning---they use the median of previously selected parameters.
\end{itemize}

On the 146-firm CapEx/Revenue panel, inner CV selects $K = 2$ in 7/8 folds and $T = 3$~years in all 8 folds (the remaining fold selects $K = 3$). The nested-CV predictive $R^2 = 0.711$ (mean across 8 CV windows) and holdout $R^2 = 0.845$ (mean of 2023--2024; per-window values 0.846 and 0.845). All headline forecast numbers in this paper use the predictive alpha $\balpha_{t|t-1}$ from this nested protocol; the holdout windows had zero exposure to any hyperparameter tuning.

\textbf{Scope condition.} The headline forecasting result is on a 146-firm US panel using CapEx/Revenue intensity. We also report structural findings (ablation, basis rotation) on a broader 93-actor panel using CapEx/Assets intensity; these use modal $R^2$ (spectral reconstruction quality) rather than predictive $R^2$ (Section~\ref{sec:pooling}).

Per-actor AR(1) is the strongest naive baseline, reflecting the moderate persistence of CapEx/Revenue ranks (median $\rho = 0.28$). Under nested CV, SMIM wins all 8 windows ($\Delta R^2 = +0.042$; bootstrap CI $[+0.031, +0.055]$; exact permutation $p = 0.004$; Table~\ref{tab:inference}). Inner CV selects $K = 2$ in 7/8 folds and $T = 3$~years in all folds. Holdout windows confirm the result ($\Delta R^2 = +0.026$, both positive, though this is smaller than the CV mean).

\textbf{PCA baselines.} Since the paper's main mechanism is cross-sectional pooling, we include two PCA-based baselines that also pool information across firms. The PCA+Kalman baseline replaces DMD with PCA eigendecomposition of the symmetric cross-sectional covariance but keeps all other pipeline components identical. The PCA projection-only baseline further removes the Kalman filter, testing whether $\hat{y}_t = \hat{\mu} + 0.99 \cdot U_{\mathrm{PCA}} U_{\mathrm{PCA}}^\top \tilde{y}_{t-1}$ suffices.

The results are informative: under nested CV, neither PCA variant significantly outperforms AR(1) ($\Delta R^2 = +0.001$ and $-0.002$, bootstrap CIs include zero, permutation $p > 0.4$). In the fixed configuration at $T = 3$yr, PCA baselines show modest gains ($+1.2$pp and $+1.0$pp) but SMIM outperforms both by $+2.2$pp (CI $[+0.014, +0.031]$, 9/10 windows). At $T = 2$yr the gap widens further: PCA gains $+2.1$pp and $+2.3$pp over AR(1), but SMIM gains $+6.6$pp, outperforming PCA by $+4.5$pp (CI $[+0.033, +0.056]$, 9/10 windows). This is consistent with an operator-specific effect: DMD captures lagged cross-sectional dynamics through temporal snapshot pairs ($X' \approx AX$) that PCA's contemporaneous covariance ($\tilde{Y}\tilde{Y}^\top/(T-1)$) misses.

We note that at $T = 2$yr, both the PCA covariance and the DMD snapshot matrices are severely underdetermined (8 snapshots for 146 variables). A truncated SVD PCA baseline---applying the same rank-$K$ SVD to the demeaned data matrix---produces numerically identical results to the standard PCA eigendecomposition at both $T = 3$yr and $T = 2$yr, ruling out implicit SVD regularisation as the source of DMD's advantage. The widening gap at shorter $T$ is therefore attributable to DMD's temporal snapshot-pair formulation, which captures lagged cross-sectional dynamics that contemporaneous covariance misses.

Richer pooled baselines (pooled AR(1) with firm fixed effects, reduced-rank VARs) are a natural direction for future work.

\textbf{Why $K = 2$.} The CapEx/Revenue cross-section is genuinely low-dimensional: two spectral modes suffice. Higher $K$ degrades performance monotonically ($K = 3$: $-0.5$pp; $K = 5$: $-1.5$pp; $K = 8$: $-5$pp), consistent with overfitting additional modes to noise. In fact, $K = 1$ produces nearly identical results to $K = 2$ across all tested configurations: at $\tau = 8$Q, $T = 2$yr, the $K = 1$ fixed-config $\Delta R^2 = +0.066$ versus $K = 2$'s $+0.066$ (difference $< 0.1$pp, both 10/10 windows). The predictive signal is thus ``nearly 1-dimensional''---a single spectral mode captures almost all exploitable cross-sectional structure. The second mode adds a negligible but consistent margin, so we retain $K = 2$.

\textbf{Holdout era.} The final 2 test windows (2023--2024) were frozen with no hyperparameter tuning exposure. \textbf{Predictive ablation on the headline panel.} Table~\ref{tab:pred_ladder} repeats the ablation on the 146-firm CapEx/Revenue panel using \emph{predictive} $R^2$ (genuine one-step-ahead forecast, $K = 2$). The pattern differs instructively from the structural ladder and warrants careful interpretation:

\emph{EWM demeaning provides the largest single predictive gain} ($+3.5$pp over the constant mean). This is not surprising: the constant per-actor mean already achieves $R^2 = 0.695$, indicating that the cross-section is persistence-dominated; an adaptive mean that tracks non-stationarity in actor positions adds substantial incremental value.

\emph{The Kalman filter on a static basis hurts} ($-2.5$pp): the fixed spectral directions become stale within the test year, and the Kalman smoothing introduces systematic error from outdated basis vectors. Online $Q$ adaptation cannot compensate ($+0.0$pp) because the problem is the basis, not the noise level.

\emph{Rolling basis update recovers the loss and adds a further $+3.4$pp}, confirming it as the essential component for predictive performance. The full pipeline's advantage over AR(1) ($+3.4$pp) is thus driven primarily by two components: adaptive demeaning and rolling basis recomputation. The incremental contribution of the Kalman filter at $K = 2$ is marginal---at this low dimensionality with quarterly basis refresh, the Kalman predict step is approximately equivalent to a basis projection (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).

The structural ladder (Table~\ref{tab:ladder}) and the predictive ablation (Table~\ref{tab:pred_ladder}) answer different questions. The structural ladder shows which components are needed to build a good spectral representation ($K = 8$, modal $R^2$); the predictive ablation shows which components drive one-step-ahead forecast accuracy ($K = 2$, predictive $R^2$). The Kalman filter is essential at $K = 8$ for regularisation but marginal at $K = 2$ where only two modes are tracked.

\textbf{Why two panels?} The structural panel (93 actors, $K = 8$, CapEx/Assets) and the headline panel (146 firms, $K = 2$, CapEx/Revenue) serve complementary roles. The structural panel's richer actor heterogeneity (mixing FRED shocks, institutional bodies, US and UK firms, and banks) and higher $K$ reveal the full dimensionality and rotation dynamics of the spectral structure---8 modes are consistently recoverable, rotating at $25.8^\circ$/quarter. The headline panel's homogeneous actor set and lower persistence ($\rho = 0.28$) provide a cleaner forecasting test where only 2 of those modes carry enough signal to predict. The structural analysis explains \emph{what the framework captures}; the predictive analysis validates \emph{that it forecasts}.

The Kalman filter \emph{hurts} because the unregularised $R$ overfits. The lower block shows the resolution: dual regularisation rescues the Kalman filter (SMIM with static basis, modal $R^2 = 0.549$), and rolling basis update captures the structural evolution that the static basis misses (modal $R^2 = 0.696$).

\subsection{Spectral Method Comparison}
\label{sec:spectral}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{img/fig5_spectral.pdf}
\caption{Spectral method comparison (descriptive): mean $R^2$ across 10 windows for five spectral decomposition methods plus DMD at $K\!=\!3$, L1 depth. The five static methods (Schur, Polar, Hermitian, Directed Variation, PCA) produce identical results because the operator is symmetric (see text). DMD outperforms because it captures lagged cross-sectional dynamics from temporal snapshot pairs.}
\label{fig:spectral}
\end{figure}

DMD ($R^2 = 0.359$) outperforms all five static-operator methods, which produce \emph{identical} results at $R^2 = 0.339$. This identity is not a coincidence: the operator is built from the Pearson cross-correlation matrix, which is \emph{exactly} symmetric by construction. For any real symmetric matrix, the Schur, Polar, Hermitian dilation, and PCA decompositions all reduce to the standard eigendecomposition---they compute the same eigenvectors and hence the same modal basis $U$. The directed variation method differs slightly in its normalisation but converges to the same subspace. This result has a clear implication: \emph{to benefit from directed spectral methods, the operator itself must be asymmetric} (e.g., from Granger causality or supply-chain linkages). When the operator is derived from contemporaneous correlation, directedness is lost before the decomposition begins.

DMD sidesteps this entirely. Rather than decomposing a static operator, it extracts modes from temporal snapshot pairs $X' \approx AX$, where the implicit operator $A$ captures \emph{lagged} cross-sectional dynamics. This operator is generically asymmetric even when the contemporaneous correlation is symmetric, explaining DMD's $+2.0$ percentage point advantage.

\subsection{Basis Rotation and Rolling Update}
\label{sec:rolling}

To isolate the rolling-vs-static mechanism, this subsection uses a fixed ($K = 8$, $T = 5$yr) configuration; the headline forecast performance remains the nested-CV result in Table~\ref{tab:baselines}.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{img/fig3_per_window.pdf}
\caption{Per-window modal $R^2$ (descriptive, 93-actor CapEx/Assets panel): rolling DMD (teal) vs static basis (dark blue). Rolling DMD wins all 10 windows, with the largest gains in windows where the static basis underperforms (W2016, W2017, W2021, W2023---periods where the cross-sectional structure shifts most from the training period). AR(1) is omitted because modal $R^2$ measures spectral reconstruction quality, not forecast accuracy, and is not directly comparable to AR(1) predictions.}
\label{fig:per_window}
\end{figure}

Tracking the DMD spectral basis across 52 rolling quarterly transitions (2010--2024) shows a pattern of gradual basis rotation (Figure~\ref{fig:rotation}). The mean principal subspace angle between successive quarterly DMD bases is $25.8^\circ$ ($\sigma = 6.5^\circ$). Quarters with larger rotation ($>32^\circ$) tend to coincide with macroeconomic events that selectively rewire sectoral relationships---the European debt crisis (2012~Q3, $41^\circ$), the US--China tariff war (2018~Q2, $37^\circ$), and the Federal Reserve's aggressive tightening cycle (2022~Q3, $38^\circ$)---though with only 52 transitions the event alignment is descriptive rather than inferential. Notably, COVID-19 (2020~Q2, $22^\circ$) did \emph{not} produce high rotation: the pandemic changed investment magnitudes across the board but preserved the cross-sectional co-movement structure, consistent with a level shock absorbed by EWM demeaning rather than a structural reorganisation of which sectors co-move.

On this 93-actor panel, the \emph{dimensionality} of the estimated spectral structure appears stable: $K_{\mathrm{eff}} = 8$ modes in all quarters, with zero mode births or deaths in this sample. (The headline CapEx/Revenue panel uses $K = 2$; the structural panel's higher $K$ is appropriate for the broader, more heterogeneous actor set.) The spectral subspace is consistently 8-dimensional, but the 8 directions gradually reorient.

\textbf{Null-simulation validation.} To assess whether the observed rotation is estimation noise, we simulate 500 panels from a \emph{fixed} latent basis (same $N$, $T$, persistence, and noise as the real data) and measure the apparent DMD rotation arising purely from finite-sample estimation. The null rotation is $75.3^\circ$ ($\sigma = 1.5^\circ$)---far \emph{above} the observed $25.8^\circ$. The intuition is straightforward: a purely noisy basis is not stable; DMD extracts different directions from each window's noise, producing nearly orthogonal subspaces and hence large apparent rotation. A genuinely structured but gradually evolving basis, by contrast, is recovered consistently across windows, producing modest rotation that reflects actual structural change. The observed $25.8^\circ$ is consistent with this second regime: reliably recoverable, low-dimensional structure that shifts gradually.

\textbf{Sensitivity.} Rotation is robust to parameter choices: it decreases smoothly with mode count ($38.6^\circ$ at $K = 3$ to $19.9^\circ$ at $K = 10$) and with training window length ($30.0^\circ$ at $T = 3$yr to $15.8^\circ$ at $T = 10$yr). Both patterns are consistent with more modes or more data producing more stable subspace estimates. The 8-mode fixed dimensionality holds across all tested configurations.

\subsection{Cross-Sectional Pooling: Training Window Effect}
\label{sec:pooling}
% ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

A distinctive property of the spectral framework is that its forecast quality degrades less than per-actor AR(1) as the training window shortens. Figure~\ref{fig:tsweep} shows $\Delta R^2$ (SMIM minus AR(1)) as a function of training window length $T$, at fixed $K = 2$ and $\tau = 12$Q. This is a \emph{fixed-configuration sweep}---not the nested-CV headline result---designed to isolate the training-window effect. At each $T$, SMIM and AR(1) use the same training window: both see data from $t - T$ to $t - 1$ and predict the 4 quarters of year $t$. AR(1) estimates per-actor $\rho$ from this window; SMIM estimates its DMD basis from the same data. Neither model receives an expanding-window advantage the other does not.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{img/fig13_tsweep.pdf}
\caption{Training window effect on forecast advantage (fixed $K\!=\!2$, $\tau\!=\!12$Q). SMIM's advantage over AR(1) increases monotonically as $T$ decreases. At $T = 2$yr, AR(1) has only 8 quarterly observations per actor to estimate $\rho$; SMIM extracts its 2-mode spectral basis from $146 \times 8 = 1{,}168$ cross-sectional observations. Error bars show 95\% bootstrap CI (10{,}000 resamples). Teal bars are significant at $p < 0.05$ (permutation test). Win counts show SMIM-vs-AR(1) per-window wins out of 10.}
\label{fig:tsweep}
\end{figure}

The pattern is \emph{consistent with} cross-sectional pooling: the DMD basis is estimated from the joint $N \times T$ panel, effectively borrowing strength across actors. Per-actor AR(1) has no mechanism for this---each actor's autoregressive coefficient is estimated from $T$ observations alone. As $T$ shrinks, the per-actor estimates degrade while the cross-sectional structure remains recoverable from the $N$-dimensional snapshots. At $T = 7$~years (28 quarters), AR(1) has sufficient data and SMIM adds nothing; at $T = 2$~years, the cross-sectional advantage produces a $+5.7$pp gain with bootstrap CI entirely above zero ($p = 0.001$). We note that this monotonic pattern is consistent with but does not prove cross-sectional pooling; alternative explanations (e.g., differential sensitivity to non-stationarity) cannot be fully excluded, though a truncated SVD PCA baseline rules out implicit regularisation as the mechanism (Section~\ref{sec:baselines}) without a formal decomposition.

\textbf{Predictive vs modal $R^2$.} The Kalman filter produces both a \emph{predictive} state $\balpha_{t|t-1} = F\balpha_{t-1|t-1}$ (using only information up to $t-1$) and a \emph{filtered} state $\balpha_{t|t}$ (incorporating the current observation $\tilde{y}_t$). All forecast comparisons and the investment gap (Eq.~\ref{eq:gap_compute}, Section~\ref{sec:d2}) use the predictive state $\balpha_{t|t-1}$. The filtered state is used for modal $R^2$ (spectral reconstruction quality) on the structural panel.

\textbf{Structural analysis panel.} The ablation ladder (Table~\ref{tab:ladder}), basis rotation analysis, variance decomposition, and CapEx revision analysis (Section~\ref{sec:d2}) use a broader 93-actor panel with $K = 8$ modes and filtered (modal) $R^2$. Modal $R^2$ measures spectral reconstruction quality---how well the DMD basis represents the current cross-section---which is the relevant metric for structural decomposition. These findings (rolling basis dominates, dual regularisation is essential, gaps are associated with CapEx revision) are structural and do not depend on the forecast comparison metric.

\section{Economic Validation}
\label{sec:economic}
% ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

\subsection{What This Gap Is and Is Not}

The investment gap $\Delta_{i,t}$ is a \emph{model-implied} deviation: the difference between observed investment intensity and a spectral-state-space benchmark (Eq.~\ref{eq:gap_compute}). It measures how far an actor's current investment rank lies from its predicted rank given the cross-sectional modal structure and its own history. This is a well-defined statistical object with demonstrated out-of-sample predictive content.

It is \emph{not}, however, a welfare-theoretic or structural misallocation measure in the sense of \citet{hsieh2009misallocation}. The benchmark is model-implied, not derived from a production-function optimality condition. A positive gap (``over-investment'') means the actor invests more than the model predicts, not that the investment is socially inefficient. We therefore use the term ``investment gap'' rather than ``misallocation'' and interpret positive economic validation results as evidence that the gap captures meaningful cross-sectional dynamics, not that it identifies welfare losses.

\subsection{Gaps and Subsequent CapEx Revision}
\label{sec:d2}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{img/fig9_d2_quintile.pdf}
\caption{Gap quintile sort (inferential): mean next-4-quarter intensity change by current gap quintile. Actors in Q5 (over-investing relative to model benchmark) reduce intensity by 0.20 rank points; Q1 (under-investing) increase by 0.19. The on-figure annotation ($\hat{\beta} = -0.27$, $t = -6.95$) is from an earlier specification; Table~\ref{tab:capex_reg} reports the final values ($\hat{\beta} = -0.279$, $t = -6.4$ with actor-clustered SEs).}
\label{fig:d2}
\end{figure}

The most informative economic validation exercise is the association between predictive investment gaps and subsequent CapEx-rank revision. The gap used here is $\Delta_{i,t} = y_{i,t} - (\hat{\mu}_i + U\balpha_{t|t-1})$, where $\balpha_{t|t-1}$ is the \emph{predictive} modal state that does not incorporate the current observation $\tilde{y}_t$. This is a genuine ex-ante gap: the benchmark $y^*_{i,t} = \hat{\mu}_i + U\balpha_{t|t-1}$ is the model's one-step-ahead forecast, and the gap measures how far the actual realisation deviates from this forecast. We estimate the panel regression:
\begin{equation}
\Delta y_{i,t \to t+4} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \Delta_{i,t} + \beta_2 y_{i,t} + \epsilon_{i,t}.
\label{eq:d2}
\end{equation}

Table~\ref{tab:capex_reg} reports six specifications on the 93-actor structural panel across 36 quarters (2015~Q1 to 2023~Q4), yielding $N = 3{,}324$ actor-quarter observations after dropping rows that lack 4 future quarters for the dependent variable. In pooled OLS with the level control: $\hat{\beta}_1 = -0.28$, $t = -6.4$ (actor-clustered SEs). Because $\Delta y_{i,t \to t+4}$ spans four overlapping quarters, consecutive observations for the same actor share three quarters of the dependent variable, inducing serial correlation. We therefore report time-clustered SEs ($t = -7.1$) as our preferred specification; the result is also robust to time fixed effects ($t = -7.1$). Actors identified as over-investing relative to the model benchmark (Q5) subsequently reduce intensity by 0.20 rank points; under-investing actors (Q1) increase by 0.19 (Figure~\ref{fig:d2}).

\textbf{The result is cross-sectional, not within-actor.} With actor FE (within-actor variation only), $\hat{\beta}_1$ flips sign to $+0.08$ ($t = +2.2$). This means the \emph{between-actor} component---which actor is above or below its model-implied benchmark---drives the main result. \emph{Within} an actor over time, gaps are associated with slight continuation rather than reversion. The pooled result is therefore a cross-sectional association (``actors that deviate more from the spectral benchmark tend to revert toward it''), not a within-actor relationship (``an actor's gap forecasts its own next move''). This limits the interpretation: the gap captures cross-sectional mean-reversion in investment ranks, not actor-level dynamics.

\subsection{Gap Persistence}

Per-actor AR(1) estimation on the gap series $\Delta_{i,t}$ yields a median autoregressive coefficient $\rho = 0.67$ and median half-life of 1.7 quarters. The following results are reported as exploratory findings that warrant further investigation. They are not part of the paper's main claims.

\subsubsection{Network Diffusion}

Layer-level gap aggregation shows a strong contemporaneous association between exogenous shocks (Layer 0) and firm-level gaps (Layer 2): $\rho = 0.86$ at 1-quarter lag ($p < 0.001$). \section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
% ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

\subsection{What Does Not Work}

\textbf{Emergence diagnostics.} On the structural panel, we tested emergence through five distinct channels: partial information decomposition (PID) synergy between spectral modes, topological data analysis (TDA) complexity, economic emergence features (cross-sectional dispersion, sector rotation velocity, Herfindahl concentration), Extended DMD (Koopman lifting with polynomial observables), and multi-resolution DMD divergence. None produces a positive $\Delta R^2$. PID and TE estimators are unreliable at $T = 20$ ($C(8,2) = 28$ mode pairs from 20 observations). Extended DMD with degree-2 polynomial lifting ($P = 44$ features) achieves modal $R^2 = 0.19$---far worse than linear DMD at $0.54$---because $T/P = 0.45$ is underdetermined. Economic emergence features are redundant with the DMD-Kalman pipeline ($\Delta R^2 = -0.003$).

\textbf{Directed spectral operators.} Transfer entropy and Granger causality operators produce genuinely asymmetric matrices (mean asymmetry $\sim 1.2$). However, the resulting spectral bases are too noisy for Kalman filtering: TE-Schur at $K = 8$ causes numerical divergence, and at $K = 3$ achieves only modal $R^2 = 0.36$ vs DMD's $0.54$.

\textbf{Event-level alignment.} Gap magnitudes do not spike around known episodes (0/8 aligned). The rank normalisation absorbs event-driven variation by construction.

\textbf{Return-based intensity.} On the structural panel, return intensity produces modal $R^2 = -0.15$ on the same US actors where CapEx/Assets intensity achieves modal $R^2 = 0.54$. The intensity construction is the primary driver of model performance.

\subsection{When the Kalman Filter Is and Is Not Needed}
\label{sec:simplification}

At $K = 2$ with quarterly basis refresh, the Kalman filter's contribution to predictive $R^2$ is marginal ($+0.3$pp). The rolling basis update reprojects the modal state into the new basis each quarter (Algorithm~\ref{alg:pipeline}), so the subsequent predict step uses $\balpha_{t|t-1} = 0.99 \cdot (U_{t-1}^\top U_{t-1})^{-1} U_{t-1}^\top \tilde{y}_{t-1}$, where $U_{t-1}$ denotes the DMD basis estimated from data through quarter $t-1$. As a heuristic approximation (omitting the Kalman gain correction and covariance update, which contribute $+0.3$pp):
\begin{equation}
\hat{y}_t \approx \hat{\mu} + 0.99 \cdot U_{t-1} (U_{t-1}^\top U_{t-1})^{-1} U_{t-1}^\top \tilde{y}_{t-1},
\label{eq:simplified}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{y}_t, \hat{\mu}, \tilde{y}_{t-1} \in \R^N$ are the full cross-sectional vectors. When $U_{t-1}$ is orthonormal, this reduces to $U_{t-1} U_{t-1}^\top \tilde{y}_{t-1}$; in general DMD modes are not orthonormal and the pseudoinverse is required. The implementation uses the simpler $U_{t-1}^\top \tilde{y}_{t-1}$ as an approximation; at $K = 2$ with quarterly refresh, the pseudoinverse and the transpose produce forecasts that differ by less than $0.01$pp in $R^2$, and all reported results use the transpose form. The actor-level forecast is $\hat{y}_{i,t} = [\hat{y}_t]_i$---a per-quarter DMD eigendecomposition followed by a matrix-vector product.

\textbf{Latent state under basis refresh.} When the rolling DMD produces a new basis $U_{\mathrm{new}}$, the implementation reprojects via $\balpha \leftarrow (U_{\mathrm{new}}^\top U_{\mathrm{new}})^{-1} U_{\mathrm{new}}^\top \tilde{y}_t$ and resets the Kalman covariance $P$ and process noise $Q$ to their defaults. This means the accumulated Kalman state is discarded at each refresh---no attempt is made to carry the state forward or smoothly interpolate between old and new bases. This is acceptable at quarterly refresh frequency because the single-step-ahead prediction uses only the reprojected state, not the full Kalman history.

The Kalman filter becomes essential in two settings: (i)~at higher $K$, where spherical $R$ prevents overfitting the $K$-dimensional observation model (the structural panel's $K = 8$ requires it; without spherical $R$, modal $R^2$ drops from $0.44$ to $-7.3$); and (ii)~when the basis is \emph{not} refreshed each quarter, where online $Q$ adaptation tracks regime shifts between basis updates ($+5.8$pp on the structural panel). These two roles---regularisation and temporal adaptation---are redundant at $K = 2$ with rolling basis, but load-bearing at higher $K$ or lower update frequency.

\textbf{Two panels, two questions.} The structural panel ($K = 8$, 93 actors, CapEx/Assets) identifies the richer rotating modal geometry and the role of dual regularisation at higher $K$: eight modes are consistently recoverable, rotating at $25.8^\circ$/quarter, and the Kalman filter's regularisation is essential. The predictive panel ($K = 2$, 146 firms, CapEx/Revenue) shows that a low-dimensional rolling variant forecasts well, with only marginal additional help from Kalman filtering. The 6 additional modes captured at $K = 8$ represent real cross-sectional structure (they survive the null-simulation test and rotate coherently), but are too noisy to improve one-step-ahead forecasts. Understanding how to exploit these additional modes for prediction is an open question.

\subsection{Reading the Spectral Structure: A Practitioner's Perspective}

The structural analysis reveals that cross-sectional investment dynamics live in a stable 8-dimensional spectral subspace that rotates continuously. We conclude the discussion by interpreting what this means concretely for someone monitoring capital allocation patterns.

\textbf{The 8 modes are investment co-movement themes.} Each DMD mode $u_k \in \R^N$ assigns a loading to every actor. Actors with same-sign loadings tend to increase or decrease their investment intensity together; opposite-sign loadings indicate substitution patterns. These are not fixed sectors---they are \emph{empirically identified} co-movement directions that cut across sector boundaries.

\textbf{$25.8^\circ$/quarter rotation means the themes drift.} Every quarter, the co-movement structure shifts: firms that invested in sync gradually decouple, while previously independent firms begin to co-move. Any model of cross-sectional investment dynamics that is not re-estimated at least quarterly will track a structure that has shifted substantially from its original orientation.

\textbf{Rotation speed is diagnostic.} During business-as-usual quarters, the basis rotates at $25.8^\circ$ ($\sigma = 6.5^\circ$). High-rotation quarters ($>35^\circ$) signal structural reorganisation: the tariff war (2018~Q2, $37^\circ$) did not merely change \emph{how much} firms invested---it changed \emph{which firms co-moved}. The European debt crisis (2012~Q3, $41^\circ$) and the Federal Reserve's tightening cycle (2022~Q3, $38^\circ$) similarly reorganised the cross-sectional pattern. This is a structural signal distinct from volatility: VIX measures how much prices move; rotation measures how the investment co-movement map rearranges.

\textbf{Stable dimensionality with shifting directions.} The number of independent co-movement themes ($K_{\mathrm{eff}} = 8$) is constant across all 52 quarterly transitions in our sample. No modes are born or die. The investment structure does not become simpler during crises or more complex during expansions---the complexity is fixed, but the directions evolve. This is consistent with a system that has a fixed number of structural degrees of freedom (sector composition, supply-chain linkages, policy transmission channels) whose relative importance shifts gradually.

\textbf{Practical implications.} (i)~Factor models for investment-related portfolios (infrastructure, CapEx-intensive sectors) must be updated at least quarterly; annual re-estimation loses substantial rotational information. (ii)~Rotation speed can serve as an early diagnostic for structural regime change---a sustained increase in rotation may signal that the investment landscape is reorganising before this becomes visible in asset prices. (iii)~The 8-dimensional structure provides a natural compression: 146 firms' investment behaviour can be summarised by 8 time-varying modal coordinates per quarter, enabling compact monitoring dashboards. (iv)~Of the 8 recoverable modes, only 2 carry enough signal for one-step-ahead prediction (Section~\ref{sec:baselines}); the remaining 6 capture real structure that is visible in reconstruction but not yet exploitable for forecasting---an open frontier for future work.

\subsection{Limitations}

\textbf{Baselines.} Beyond the PCA baselines reported in Table~\ref{tab:baselines}, a pooled AR(1) with firm fixed effects, reduced-rank VARs, and standard Kalman-smoothed dynamic factor models are natural additional comparators that would further clarify the source of the framework's advantage. A truncated PCA baseline receiving the same rank-$K$ SVD produces numerically identical results to the standard PCA eigendecomposition (Section~\ref{sec:baselines}), ruling out SVD regularisation as the explanation for DMD's advantage.

\textbf{Panel scope.} Both panels (93-actor structural and 146-firm headline) are US-dominated, with limited international evidence. UK results are weak (modal $R^2 = 0.058$ on the structural panel) due to the return-based intensity method, not geography per se. Extending EDGAR-equivalent coverage to international markets would test whether the framework generalises beyond the US CapEx setting.

\textbf{Inference.} The 4-quarter test windows provide noisy $R^2$ estimates per window. Window-level inference with 8--10 observations has limited power; the bootstrap and exact permutation tests are the primary inferential objects. The training-window sweep (Figure~\ref{fig:tsweep}) demonstrates a consistent monotonic pattern across five $T$ values, providing additional corroboration.

\textbf{Economic validation.} The gap-revision regression uses the predictive state $\balpha_{t|t-1}$ (which does not incorporate the current observation), providing a genuine ex-ante gap measure. The result is cross-sectional (it flips sign under actor fixed effects), not within-actor predictive.

The intensity rank normalisation, while stabilising the panel, absorbs event-level information and limits the model's utility for crisis detection or timing applications.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

We present a spectral state-space framework for forecasting cross-sectional investment intensity. On a 146-firm US CapEx/Revenue panel, a 2-mode DMD basis with rolling recomputation achieves predictive $R^2 = 0.711$ under nested CV (8 windows; $\Delta R^2 = +0.042$ vs AR(1); bootstrap CI $[+0.031, +0.055]$; exact permutation $p = 0.004$) and $R^2 = 0.845$ on 2 frozen holdout windows ($\Delta R^2 = +0.026$, both positive). A PCA baseline using the same pipeline but replacing DMD with eigendecomposition of the sample covariance achieves $R^2 = 0.702$---statistically indistinguishable from AR(1)---suggesting the advantage is consistent with DMD's temporal snapshot formulation rather than cross-sectional pooling alone; a truncated SVD PCA baseline rules out implicit SVD regularisation as the source. The result rests on three principles:

\emph{Dual regularisation}: both the observation covariance ($R = cI$, replacing $N^2$ parameters) and the transition matrix ($F = 0.99I$, replacing $K^2$ parameters) must be aggressively regularised. EM estimation overfits both; fixed scalars with online adaptation outperform in every window.

\emph{Temporal basis tracking}: on the 93-actor structural panel ($K = 8$), the spectral basis rotates at $25.8^\circ$ per quarter ($\sigma = 6.5^\circ$) with stable dimensionality. This rotation, missed by a static basis, accounts for the single largest modal $R^2$ gain ($+14.7$pp, CI $[+11.1, +17.2]$pp). Standard Kalman filtering updates states within the basis but cannot track the basis itself.

\emph{Two complementary panels}: the structural panel ($K = 8$, 93 actors, CapEx/Assets) identifies the richer rotating modal geometry and the role of dual regularisation; the predictive panel ($K = 2$, 146 firms, CapEx/Revenue) shows that a low-dimensional rolling variant forecasts well. On the predictive panel, EWM demeaning provides the largest single gain ($+3.5$pp), with rolling basis update adding a further $+3.4$pp; the Kalman filter's incremental contribution at $K = 2$ is marginal (Section~\ref{sec:simplification}).

Predictive model-implied gap estimates (using $\balpha_{t|t-1}$) are associated with subsequent cross-sectional CapEx-rank revision ($t \approx -6.4$ to $-7.1$ with clustered SEs), though this is a between-actor effect that reverses under actor fixed effects (Table~\ref{tab:capex_reg}). The transition dynamics use stable default scalars ($F = 0.99I$, $Q = 0.5I$) that performed consistently across all evaluation windows in this sample---only the spectral basis requires periodic re-estimation.

\textbf{Cross-sectional pooling.} In a fixed-configuration sweep, the framework's advantage increases monotonically as the training window shortens, from near-zero at $T = 7$~years to $\Delta R^2 = +0.057$ at $T = 2$~years (10/10 windows; Figure~\ref{fig:tsweep}). This pattern is consistent with the spectral basis exploiting $N \times T$ cross-sectional observations collectively, while per-actor AR(1) is limited to each actor's own history.

\textbf{Limitations.} Beyond the PCA baselines in Table~\ref{tab:baselines}, richer pooled benchmarks (panel AR with firm fixed effects, reduced-rank VARs) have not been tested. The economic validation uses predictive gaps ($\balpha_{t|t-1}$), which is clean ex-ante, but the result is cross-sectional (flips under actor FE), not within-actor predictive. The holdout advantage ($\Delta R^2 = +0.026$) is smaller than the CV mean ($+0.042$), though with only 2 holdout windows this is uninformative.

\textbf{Scope conditions.} The headline predictive result is on a 146-firm US CapEx/Revenue panel. The framework also beats AR(1) on Revenue/Assets (259 firms, $+1.0$pp) and a multi-ratio panel (270 firms, $+2.8$pp), confirming it is not specific to a single intensity construction (Table~\ref{tab:multi_signal}). The structural findings (ablation, rotation, economic validation) are established on a broader 93-actor CapEx/Assets panel using modal (filtered) reconstruction. Return-based intensity (UK equities) and asset-growth intensity (banks) produce weak or negative $R^2$---the framework requires sufficient cross-sectional dynamics (low persistence). The PCA cross-sectional baseline is consistent with a DMD-specific advantage, but richer pooled benchmarks (panel regressions with firm fixed effects, reduced-rank VARs) are a natural direction for future work. Generalisation to international markets and sub-quarterly frequencies remains open. The methodological lesson---dual regularisation and basis tracking for high-dimensional state-space models---extends beyond this specific application.
